# Writing the chapters

## Anatomy of a chapter

- **Era label:** a year or range ("1845", "1902–1931", "Since the mid-1970s", "Today").
- **Title:** two to four words ("The Canal Comes Through", "Frogtown and
  Cheesequarters").
- **Text:** two or three short paragraphs, about 100–170 words in all. One idea per
  paragraph. It has to fit under the image on a phone, so long chapters are a layout
  problem as well as a reading one.
- **Credit line:** "Photo: <what it shows>, <date>. <Archive name>". Use "Drawing:",
  "Map:" or "Postcard:" for those.

Open the site with a title over the first chapter. End with the present day, an
invitation (visit the museum, walk the trail), and a colophon. The colophon says:
- where the images and facts came from;
- how the images were treated, and anything removed (such as "handwritten captions on two
  photographs");
- that the site isn't affiliated with the archive, if it isn't.

## Voice

Write like a good local guide: warm, concrete, specific.
- Use names, numbers and small vivid details ("all six families lived at first in a
  twelve-by-fourteen-foot log hut").
- A little wit and an occasional editorial aside are welcome when they're true and earned
  ("Neighbors called the east side Frogtown").
- Steer away from:
  - asides that read oddly out of context ("The land is still this flat");
  - stock flourishes ("a testament to", "rich tapestry", "stood the test of time");
  - present-tense claims you can't keep true ("still the largest", "still family-run")
    unless a current source supports them.

## The accuracy pass

Before showing a draft, go sentence by sentence:
1. Find each fact's line in `sources.md`. If it isn't there, find a source, mark it as
   general context, or cut it.
2. Where sources disagree, use the softened wording recorded in the ledger.
3. Check that each paraphrase still means what the source said. "One of the first
   non-log-cabin structures in the original plat" can become "one of the first houses in
   town not built of logs", but not "the first brick house".
4. Check summary claims: counts of images, "every photograph", "the oldest".
5. Check that the credit matches the image and gives its real date.

## Editing without flattening

When the person points at a phrase they dislike:
- Fix that phrase, then look for others like it: the same kind of awkwardness, not
  everything you'd write differently.
- Show what you changed: before and after for each edit, briefly.
- Keep their favorite lines unless they're wrong.
- If an edit would remove an opinion or a piece of character, ask first.

When they ask for something more specific ("say it turned the antenna toward the radio
and TV stations"), make that change in their words as closely as fits the sentence.

## Sensitive history

Decide with the person what the story includes:
- disasters, epidemics and riots;
- discrimination;
- the Native nations who lived on or used the land first.

An upbeat community site can leave a flood out. Record the choice and the reason under
"Left out on purpose" in `sources.md`, so it's a decision rather than an accident.

Don't erase anyone. If the land had earlier inhabitants or trails, say so accurately,
with sources, even in a brief "before the town" chapter.

## Alt text

The visible credit and the alt text can share a source, but the alt text starts with the
kind of image: "Photograph: Strawberry fields at the Walnut Hill Fruit Farm, 1909." This
lets screen-reader users know a drawing is a drawing. Describe what's visible. Don't
explain the chapter.
